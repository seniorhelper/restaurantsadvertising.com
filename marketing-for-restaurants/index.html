Timing beats budget

The restaurant marketing year

Most restaurants market hardest when they are already busy, because that is when there is money to spend. It is precisely backwards.

The work that fills February has to happen in January. The work that fills a Tuesday has to happen the week before, not that afternoon. Marketing has a lag, and restaurants that ignore it spend money to make busy nights slightly busier.

The short version

What is restaurant marketing?

Restaurant marketing is everything you do to get people to walk in, choose you, and come back. It breaks into three jobs. Getting found covers search, maps, AI assistants, directories and word of mouth. Getting chosen covers your reviews, photos, menu and website — the things someone checks in the ninety seconds between finding you and deciding. Getting them back covers email, loyalty, service and the reasons a guest returns instead of trying somewhere new. Most restaurants spend almost everything on the first job and almost nothing on the third, which is exactly the wrong ratio.

Here is the thing nobody says out loud: a new guest costs far more to acquire than a returning one costs to keep. Yet the marketing plan at most restaurants is entirely about strangers.

This page covers all three jobs, in order, with what to do and roughly what it costs. Start wherever your weakest link is.

The framework

The three jobs, and where restaurants break

Diagnosing which job is failing saves you from spending money on the wrong one. Almost every struggling restaurant has a clear weak link, and it is usually not the one the owner thinks.

1
Get found

Do people deciding where to eat ever see you?

This is search, maps, AI assistants, directories, signage and word of mouth. If you are invisible here, nothing else you do matters — the best food in the city loses to the restaurant that shows up in the map pack.

How to tell this is your problem: your regulars love you, your reviews are strong, but volume is flat and nobody new comes in. You are running a good restaurant that the market cannot find.

Where to start: your Google Business Profile, then your menu. Our restaurant SEO guide covers this layer in depth.

2
Get chosen

Do they pick you once they see you?

Someone found you. Now they spend about ninety seconds deciding. They scan your star rating, read three recent reviews, look at photos, glance at the menu and check the price range. Every one of those is a filter you either pass or fail.

The bar has moved sharply. 31% of consumers will now only use a business rated 4.5 stars or higher, up from 17% a year earlier, and 68% require at least 4 stars. 47% will not use a business with fewer than 20 reviews.

How to tell this is your problem: your listing gets plenty of views but few calls or direction requests. People are finding you and walking past.

3
Get them back

Do they return without you paying again?

The most ignored job and the most profitable one. A returning guest costs almost nothing to reach if you have their email, and they spend more because they already trust you.

How to tell this is your problem: steady covers but no recognizable faces, and a permanent feeling of running on a treadmill. You are renting customers instead of keeping them.

Where to start: capture email addresses, then use them. Most restaurants have no list at all, which means every single guest has to be won again from scratch.

Fix them in order

There is no point building a loyalty program if nobody can find you, and no point buying ads if your rating is 3.8 and your last photo is from 2022. Work left to right. Each job feeds the next, and the earlier ones are almost always cheaper to fix than the later ones.

The planner further down asks about all three and tells you which one to attack first.

The list

28 restaurant marketing ideas that actually work

Grouped by what they cost and how fast they pay back. Start at the top. Nothing in the first group costs money, and the first group is where most of the return lives.

Free, and fast

1. Claim and complete your Google Business Profile

Just 35% of small businesses have one. Categories, hours, attributes, photos, menu link. Highest-return hour in restaurant marketing, full stop.

2. Set holiday hours a year ahead

Fifteen minutes in January prevents a year of families driving to a locked door and leaving one-star reviews about it.

3. Ask every happy guest for a review

78% of consumers were asked last year and 83% of those asked wrote one. Asking is the whole tactic. Build it into the check drop.

4. Answer every review within a week

80% of consumers prefer businesses that respond to all reviews. 19% now expect a same-day reply, up from 6%. Keep replies short and specific.

5. Claim Apple Business Connect

Apple Maps usage nearly doubled from 14% to 27% in a year. Almost no restaurant has claimed it. Free, twenty minutes.

6. Put your menu online as text

Turns forty dishes into forty findable phrases. The single biggest visibility change available to most restaurants.

7. Photograph your food properly

Daylight, near a window, phone camera, ten shots a month. Photo views are a ranking signal and the first thing a stranger judges.

8. Start collecting emails at the table

A card, a tablet, a line on the check. Every address you capture is a guest you never have to pay to reach again.

Cheap, and steady

9. Run a real email list

Two sends a month. One about food, one about something happening. Not coupons every week — that trains people to wait for discounts.

10. Build a slow-night ritual

Not a discount. A reason. Trivia, a guest chef, industry night, a regional menu. Discounts fill seats once. Rituals fill them every week.

11. Partner with neighboring businesses

The theater, the gym, the brewery, the hotel front desk. Cross-referrals cost nothing and send people who already trust the referrer.

12. Get on the local guides and directories

Not four hundred junk ones. The ten your city actually uses. These are also the sources AI assistants pull from when recommending.

13. Make your window work

A tap-or-scan menu tag on the glass lets passersby read your entire menu without opening the door. Nearly nobody does this.

14. Thank people who post about you

Comment, thank, ask permission to reuse the clip. Video platforms are a rising recommendation channel and creators respond to being noticed.

15. Train the floor to sell honestly

A server who can recommend a starter and pair a glass raises check average with zero ad spend. It is a trainable skill, not a personality.

16. Write occasion pages

Private dining, large groups, gluten free, patio, late night. Far less competition than your cuisine plus your city, and much higher intent.

17. Run a birthday club

One field at signup. An automatic email two weeks out. Birthdays bring tables of six, not tables of one, and the guest of honor never pays alone.

18. Fix your mobile site speed

73% of local searches start on a phone. Hours, phone, address and menu should be one tap away with nothing covering the screen.

Costs money, worth it when the basics are done

19. Rebuild the website properly

Fast, mobile-first, readable menu, clean schema, one-tap calling. This is where the decision closes — 54% visit the site after reading good reviews, up from 32% in 2019.

20. NFC and QR menus

Instant updates, live availability, dietary filters, and a menu machines can read. See how NFC menus work.

21. Professional photography, once

Twenty hero shots you own forever. Use phone photos for volume and freshness, professional ones for the listing and the website.

22. Local sponsorship with a real audience

The youth league, the neighborhood festival, the theater program. Choose based on whether their audience eats near you, not on the price.

23. Paid search for your gaps

Useful for the terms you cannot rank for yet, and for defending your own brand name. Not a substitute for being findable organically.

24. Direct ordering that you own

Cuts marketplace commission and gives you the customer's information. At a 4% margin, a dollar saved on commission equals roughly $25 of new dine-in revenue.

Longer plays that compound

25. Become the expert on something

One dish, one region, one technique. Being the definitive local answer for a specific thing beats being an option for a broad one, in search and in conversation.

26. Build a genuine private events business

High ticket, booked in advance, fills the nights nobody walks in. It needs a real page, real photos and a person who answers inquiries fast.

27. Get into the AI answers

Usage for local recommendations went 6% to 45% in a year. There is no ad slot to buy — only consistent, verifiable, well-described information.

28. Turn regulars into a community

Learn names. Remember orders. A restaurant people feel ownership of does not need much marketing, because the guests do it.

Do not do all 28

Pick three. Finish them. Then pick three more. The restaurants that fail at marketing are almost never the ones that did too little — they are the ones that started nine things and completed none of them while the kitchen was on fire.

        The money question

        How much should a restaurant spend on marketing?

        The common industry guidance is roughly 3% to 6% of revenue, with newer restaurants at the higher end while they build awareness and established ones at the lower end. But the percentage is the least useful part of the answer. What matters far more is the order you spend in, because the first several thousand dollars of work in most restaurants is free or nearly free — and skipping it to buy ads is how marketing budgets get wasted.

        Spend in this order

        • Everything free first. Profile, hours, photos, reviews, text menu. Costs time, not money. Do not skip to step two.
        • Then the website. This is where the decision closes and where your menu lives as readable text.
        • Then owned channels. Email capture and direct ordering. These lower your future acquisition cost permanently.
        • Then reach. Paid search, sponsorship, promotion. Only once the first three can convert what they send you.

        Buying reach before you can convert it is the most common and most expensive mistake in restaurant marketing. You pay to send strangers to a 3.8-star listing with three-year-old photos, and they choose someone else.

        Judge every dollar against one number

        What is one additional cover worth to you? Take your average check and your margin, and you have the number that makes every marketing decision straightforward.

        A campaign that costs $500 and brings twelve tables of two at a $30 average check produced $720. Whether that was worth it depends on your margin — and on whether any of those twenty-four people came back.

        That second question is where almost all the real return lives, and almost nobody measures it. Run the numbers on the empty table calculator if you have not already.

        The most ignored math

        Why keeping a guest beats finding one

        Restaurants obsess over new customers and quietly let existing ones drift. The arithmetic says that is backwards, and it is not close.

        Consider a guest who comes once. At a $32 average check, they are worth $32 and you spent something to reach them.

        Now consider the same guest coming once a month for two years. That is $768, and after the first visit you spent almost nothing — an email you were already sending, a server who remembered their name.

        Same person. Twenty-four times the value. The difference was not a better ad. It was whether you had any way to reach them again and any reason for them to come back.

        This is why email capture sits so high on the idea list despite being unglamorous. A list of two thousand past guests is a marketing channel you own outright, that costs almost nothing to use, and that no algorithm change can take away from you.

        It is also why service quality is a marketing line item, not an operations one. The cheapest marketing in the industry is a guest who leaves saying they will be back and means it.

        Reasons regulars stop coming

        Ranked roughly by how often we hear them, and notice how few are about food.

        • Nothing changed on the menu and they got bored.
        • One bad service experience that nobody acknowledged.
        • A favorite dish disappeared without explanation.
        • Prices moved and nobody said why.
        • They moved jobs or neighborhoods and simply forgot.
        • A new place opened and asked for their attention while you did not.

        Four of those six are solvable with an email list and a rotating special. The last one is solvable by asking.

        Channels nobody can take away

        Email, SMS and the case for owning your audience

        Social platforms change their algorithms and your reach evaporates overnight. Delivery apps hold your customer list hostage. An email list is yours permanently.

        Email

        The workhorse. Two sends a month is plenty. One about the food — a new dish, a seasonal change, something the chef is excited about. One about an event or a reason to come in. Keep it short, make it feel like a person wrote it, and resist making every send a discount.

        SMS

        Much higher open rates and much lower tolerance for misuse. Use it sparingly and only for things that are genuinely time-sensitive: a last-minute table release, a one-night event, a weather-driven special. Requires explicit consent and a clear opt-out, and the rules are strict.

        Your own ordering

        Every direct order builds your list while saving commission. This is the compounding version of owning your audience, because the channel pays for itself and grows the asset at the same time.

        How to actually collect addresses

        • A line on the check with a QR or tap tag.
        • Wifi signin that asks for an email once.
        • The reservation system, if you use one.
        • Direct online orders, automatically.
        • A birthday club card at the host stand.
        • Anyone who books a private event or large party.

        None of these require a campaign. They require deciding to do it and then not stopping.

        What to send that people open

        • Behind the scenes on a new dish, with a real photo.
        • A short note when something seasonal arrives.
        • Early access to holiday reservations before the public.
        • An honest heads-up when something changes.
        • Genuine thanks after a busy stretch.

        The test: would you be glad to receive it? If it reads as a coupon blast, it will be treated as one, and eventually people stop opening anything you send.

        Rising fast

        Social media and video for restaurants

        Short video is now a recommendation channel, not just a branding one. YouTube, Instagram and TikTok all gained ground as places people go for local business recommendations, and TikTok has launched a Local Explorer program mirroring Google Local Guides.

        That said, be clear about what social does and does not do. It does not directly move your search rankings. It does build recognition, it does convert people who are already considering you, and it does occasionally produce a video that fills your dining room for a month.

        The realistic approach for a working restaurant: post two or three times a week, mostly food, shot on a phone in daylight. Reply to comments. Thank people who post about you and ask permission to reuse their clips — user content outperforms polished brand content almost every time, and it costs you nothing.

        What to avoid is treating social as the whole marketing plan. A restaurant with eight thousand followers and an unclaimed Google profile has its priorities exactly inverted.

        What performs, honestly

        • Close, well-lit food. Cheese pulls, pours, sizzle, steam.
        • The kitchen actually working.
        • A person talking about one dish for twenty seconds.
        • Something arriving seasonally.
        • Regulars and staff, with permission.

        What does not

        • Stock photos of food you do not serve.
        • Graphics announcing that it is Friday.
        • Reposted quotes about loving food.
        • Long gaps followed by six posts in a day.
        • Anything that reads as an ad rather than as your restaurant.
        Save your money

        Where restaurant marketing money gets wasted

        We would rather you spend nothing than spend it on these. Every one is something we have watched restaurants pay for repeatedly.

        Deep discount deal sites

        They fill seats once with people optimizing for price, at a loss, and they rarely return at full price. If you need volume urgently there are cheaper ways to get it that do less brand damage.

        Buying followers or reviews

        Fake reviews now carry real legal exposure in the US under the FTC rule, violate every platform's terms, and 97% of consumers think businesses should face consequences for them. The downside is catastrophic and the upside is imaginary.

        Mass directory submission

        Four hundred listings with slightly wrong details actively damages the consistency that both Google and AI models rely on. Ten accurate citations beat four hundred sloppy ones every time.

        Ads pointing at a broken destination

        Paying to send people to a slow site with a PDF menu and a 3.8-star rating. You are buying traffic for a page designed to lose it. Fix the destination first.

        Print with no way to measure

        Not that print never works — local publications with the right audience sometimes do well. But run it with a trackable offer or a dedicated phone number so you find out. Local news sites as a recommendation source fell from 48% to 29% in a year, so verify the audience is still there.

        Agencies reporting on impressions

        If the monthly report leads with impressions, reach and follower growth rather than calls, direction requests and covers, you are paying for a slideshow. Ask for the numbers that connect to people sitting down.

        Cheapest reach there is

        Local partnerships and community marketing

        A referral from a business your guest already trusts converts better than any ad, and costs nothing but a conversation.

        Who to talk to first

        Look for businesses that share your customer but not your revenue. They have no reason to compete with you and every reason to help.

        • Hotels and short-term rental hosts. Front desks get asked "where should we eat" constantly. A stack of cards and one good meal for the staff goes a very long way.
        • Theaters, venues and galleries. Pre-show and post-show traffic is predictable, time-boxed and hungry.
        • Gyms and studios. Especially for breakfast, smoothies or anything that fits how their members eat.
        • Breweries and bars without kitchens. A natural pairing that helps both sides.
        • Offices and coworking spaces nearby. Lunch volume and catering, which is high-ticket and repeats.
        • Neighborhood associations and schools. Slower to pay off, but it produces the kind of loyalty advertising cannot buy.

        How to actually ask

        Walk in. Not an email. Bring something. Ask what they need rather than opening with what you want, and make the first offer one-sided in their favor.

        A hotel front desk does not want your flyer. They want to look good to a guest who asked a question. Give them something that makes the recommendation easy: a card with your best three dishes, a note that you will hold a table for their guests, a direct line to the manager.

        Then feed the staff. The single most effective hotel partnership tactic is a free meal for the front desk team. They cannot recommend food they have never tasted, and once they have tasted it they recommend it with conviction for years.

        Track it loosely with a code word, a specific dish comp or a note at the host stand. You do not need attribution software, just a rough sense of which partners are actually sending people.

        The version that fails

        Printing a thousand flyers and dropping them at businesses that never agreed to anything. Nobody hands out a flyer they were not asked about, and the stack ends up in a drawer. Partnerships are relationships, which means a person has to actually have a conversation with another person. That is the cost, and it is the reason most restaurants skip it, and it is exactly why it still works.

        Know what worked

        How to measure restaurant marketing

        You do not need an analytics stack. You need five numbers and the discipline to look at them monthly.

        Calls from your listing

        Free in your Google Business Profile insights. The closest free proxy for a booking that exists.

        Direction requests

        Someone asking how to get to your door is about as high-intent as a signal gets.

        Menu views

        Whether people get far enough to seriously consider you. A leading indicator of covers.

        New vs returning guests

        Your POS can usually tell you. The ratio reveals which of the three jobs is failing better than any other single number.

        Review count and recency

        Track new reviews per month, not lifetime total. 74% of consumers only care about the last three months.

        AI mention rate

        Manual and monthly. Ask three assistants for the best in your category and city, log whether you appear. Nobody else is tracking this yet.

        Compare to last year, not last month

        Restaurants are seasonal, weather-sensitive and event-driven. A 20% jump in August proves nothing on its own, because August is not February. Compare the same period year over year wherever you have the data, and be suspicious of anyone presenting a month-over-month gain as proof their work caused it.

        Good measurement is patient and admits what it cannot prove. That is not a weaker claim — it is the only honest one.

        Opening, or newly open

        Marketing a brand new restaurant

        The single most valuable thing you can do before opening is claim your Google Business Profile early and get it complete. New restaurants have no reviews, no citations and no history, which means machines have almost nothing to go on. Starting the profile weeks before service gives Google time to establish you as a real place with a real address, so opening week traffic has somewhere to land.

        The second is to plan your first reviews deliberately. A restaurant with zero reviews fails the filter for 47% of consumers, who will not use a business with fewer than 20. Getting the first twenty honestly and quickly matters more in month one than anything else you could spend money on.

        Soft openings are the natural mechanism. Invite neighbors, nearby business owners and friends of staff, feed them well, and ask every single one of them. Do not offer anything in exchange, and do not ask for a specific rating — just ask honestly for the review.

        The third is to resist the urge to buy reach in week one. It is the most tempting moment and the worst time, because you have no reviews to convert the traffic and your operation is still finding its rhythm. Spend that money in month three when you can actually hold what you catch.

        And take the failure-rate myth off your shoulders while you are at it. The commonly repeated claim that 90% of restaurants fail in year one has no research behind it — the actual figure in the leading study is around 26%, and a Bureau of Labor Statistics analysis puts it near 17%, lower than other service businesses.         Restaurant marketing questions

        What is the best marketing for a restaurant?

        For almost every restaurant, the highest-return marketing is a complete Google Business Profile plus a steady flow of recent reviews. It is free, it takes about an hour to set up, and it reaches people at the exact moment they are choosing where to eat. Just 35% of small businesses have claimed a profile at all. Everything else — ads, social, events, partnerships — works better once that foundation exists and performs poorly without it.

        How much should a restaurant spend on marketing?

        Common industry guidance is 3% to 6% of revenue, with newer restaurants at the higher end. But the order you spend in matters far more than the percentage. Do everything free first: profile, hours, photos, reviews, text menu. Then the website. Then owned channels like email and direct ordering. Only then buy reach. Paying for traffic before you can convert it is the most expensive mistake in restaurant marketing.

        How do I get more customers in my restaurant?

        Work the three jobs in order. First get found: claim your Google profile, put your menu online as real text, get listed where people and AI assistants look. Then get chosen: fresh photos, recent reviews, a rating above 4.5, a fast mobile site. Then get them back: capture emails and give people a reason to return. Most restaurants have a clear weak link, and fixing it is far cheaper than spending on all three at once.

        How do I fill slow nights at my restaurant?

        Build a ritual rather than a discount. Trivia, a guest chef, industry night, a regional menu, live music — something that gives people a reason to come specifically on that night. Discounts fill seats once and train guests to wait for the next one. Rituals fill the same night every week and build a community around it. Then tell your email list about it, because the people most likely to come are the ones who already like you.

        Does social media actually help restaurants?

        Yes, but not in the way most people assume. Social posts are not a direct search ranking factor. What social does is build recognition and convert people already considering you, and short video is now a genuine recommendation channel — YouTube, Instagram and TikTok all gained ground as places people find local businesses. Post two or three times a week, mostly food shot on a phone in daylight. Just do not let it replace claiming your Google profile.

        Should I use deal sites and deep discounts?

        Generally no. They fill seats once with people optimizing for price, usually at a loss, and those guests rarely return at full price. They can also anchor your perceived value lower permanently. If you need volume urgently, a themed night, a partnership with a nearby business or an email to your existing guests will cost less and do less brand damage.

        How do I get more Google reviews?

        Ask, at the moment a guest says the meal was good. 78% of consumers were asked for a review in the past year and 83% of those asked wrote one. Make it one tap from the check or a table card. Never offer anything in exchange — incentivized reviews violate platform rules and now carry legal exposure in the US. Then respond to every review, briefly and specifically, because 42% of consumers are unlikely to use a business that never replies.

        Do I need a website if I have social media and a Google profile?

        Yes. 54% of consumers visit a business website after reading positive reviews, up from 32% in 2019 — the site is where the decision closes. It is also the only place your full menu lives as readable text you control, which is what makes dish-level search and AI recommendations possible. Only about 40% of local businesses have a dedicated site, which is precisely why having a good one is still an advantage.

        How long does restaurant marketing take to work?

        It varies enormously by tactic. Google profile fixes can move within days. Email to an existing list works the same week. Review building compounds over months. Search rankings for competitive terms take three to six months. AI visibility is the least predictable because models refresh on their own schedules. Plan on ninety days before judging anything structural, and compare against the same period last year rather than last month.

        Is it worth hiring a restaurant marketing agency?

        Only after you have done the free work, and only if they report on numbers that connect to covers. If a proposal leads with impressions, reach or follower growth rather than calls, direction requests and returning guests, keep looking. A good agency should be willing to tell you which items you could handle yourself — and if the honest answer is that you need three afternoons rather than a retainer, that is what they should say.
